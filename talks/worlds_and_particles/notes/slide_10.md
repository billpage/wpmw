This slide only exists for two or more particles, and it's where I think the formal analogy hides the most.

The picture is the configuration plane of two particles on a line, with the first particle's position across and the second's up. A whole world is a single point in this plane. The dashed diagonal is contact, where the two positions are equal. Two worlds, w, filled, a positon, and w′, a ring, a negaton, are joined by a chord, in blue, with its reader at the midpoint.

The chord has a part along the centre-of-mass direction and a part along the bond direction, the direction of the relative coordinate. For a pair potential, the potential difference across the chord depends only on the bond part, so the centre-of-mass part is dark: nothing there is read. The reader is a world reading the relative direction of its own configuration space, not either world-particle, and not the centre of mass.

For N particles this generalises exactly. That's Proposition WC9, and we re-checked it symbolically for three particles. The compensated residual is a sum over the N(N − 1)/2 bonds of a single function of two variables, the bond's separation and the chord's extent along it, and a trap, or any quadratic term, drops out. Four things follow. Each channel of a bond moves the two particles' momenta by equal and opposite amounts, so total momentum is conserved. A world carries one bank per bond, and identical particles share one kernel table. If the pair potential is quadratic, every kernel vanishes and nothing fires. And a chord that moves only some of the particles reads exactly the terms of the potential that involve those particles: for three particles with one-, two- and three-body terms, a chord on particle 1 reads its own one-body term, its two bonds and the three-body term, and nothing else.

The VLBI analogy from step 25 works at two levels here. Between worlds, a chord is a baseline, and the reader is the correlator: it belongs to neither station, and exists only for the pair. Within one world, the banks are counted like the baselines of an array: N particles have as many bonds as N stations have baselines. At both levels the information is carried by a pair, never by one member.
