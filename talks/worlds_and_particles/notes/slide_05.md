This slide is the heart of the confusion, and it's a confusion of notation more than of physics.

For two particles in one world, the natural coordinates are the centre of mass, X, the average of the two positions, with the total momentum P; and the relative coordinate r, the difference of the two positions, with the relative momentum, half the difference of the two momenta. That's a bond. It's classical: the potential of r gives the Newtonian force on both world-particles, and the bond is also the direction along which events move momentum.

For two worlds of one particle, the natural coordinates of a chord are the midpoint X and the half-separation Y: the worlds sit at X plus Y and X minus Y. Those are the coordinates of the Wigner transform, where the density matrix is evaluated at X plus Y and X minus Y. The misalignment of the two clocks, carried to a common point by the lever P, is the μ written in the right-hand box. That's a relation between worlds.

The two kinds of pair have exactly the same coordinate formulas. So when your Volterra-sea pair has a centre of mass and a relative coordinate, or when your bound-pair chemistry gives a P–N pair a Morse potential in its separation, the formulas fit either reading. With one particle there's no way to tell them apart, because there are no bonds at all. With two particles there is: the bond lives inside one row of the table on slide 4, and the chord lives between two rows.

The identity at the bottom of the slide was checked symbolically. Write the von Neumann equation in position representation: the time derivative of the density matrix, as a function of x and x prime, is given by H at x minus H at x prime. That's a Schrödinger equation in two coordinates, whose Hamiltonian has the usual kinetic term in x, the same kind of term in x prime but with mass minus m, and the potential V at x minus V at x prime. So the second coordinate evolves under minus H. Mass minus m in the potential minus V is just what minus H looks like for a particle in a potential; the general statement is that it's the same system run backward in time. It isn't a particle, and it isn't a world on its own. The pair, x and x prime, is a chord: two worlds, whose phase is the difference of their actions. In the midpoint and the half-difference, that difference is the chord action of slide 8.

This is the doubled space of Liouville-space methods, and of the Schwinger-Keldysh closed time path, where the bra arm is the backward branch, and the midpoint and the half-difference are its classical and quantum coordinates. Thermo-field dynamics, from Takahashi and Umezawa in 1975, uses the same doubling, with a tilde copy of the system that carries minus H. But its state vector is the square root of the density matrix, so its expectations are quadratic, like the Born rule. Ours are linear in the density matrix, as they are in Liouville space.

Three cautions follow. The squared magnitude of the density matrix at x and x prime isn't a probability: the weight of worlds sits on the diagonal, and off the diagonal the density matrix carries the phase relations between them. The density matrix is defined on every pair of configurations, occupied or not, so it doesn't by itself say there are worlds at both ends of a chord; that's the open question we'll meet on slide 9. And the minus sign on the bra copy isn't what makes negatons. Negative values of W come from interference, in the Fourier transform over Y.

I first noticed this in September, when I wrote that the ket arm sits at x plus y and the bra arm at x minus y: that's a separation of worlds, not of particles. With N particles the doubled space has two copies of 3 N dimensions, and the chord Y is a vector with one part for each particle.

One caution, from the summary of our calls on the 13th and 20th. If the separation of a P–N pair is to play the chord's role, it has to range up to about the reach, because the kernel needs coherence over a wavelength of the potential. A spring confined inside one cell can't be the chord.
