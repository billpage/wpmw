This slide answers a question I raised after reading Schiff and Poirier: is our machinery between worlds more complicated than theirs? The answer rests on one distinction, between a label and a coordinate.

A model of many worlds is a map from the set of worlds, and time, into configuration space. The coordinates of the domain, the label and the time, are what the action integrates over. The coordinates of the target, the positions, are what it varies. A label is constant along each world and is never observed, except through the density it pushes forward. The distinction is about role, not kind: in the Eulerian description the two swap, and in parametrised mechanics even time becomes a dependent variable. It's the distinction between material and spatial coordinates in continuum mechanics, and within one formulation it's fixed.

On the left, Schiff and Poirier. Their worlds are a smooth one-parameter family, labelled by C, with C chosen so that the density is uniform in C; in one dimension the density is then one over the derivative of position with respect to C. The velocity has one value per position, so only pure states can be described. Up to a total derivative, their quantum term is the Fisher information of the density, and its Euler-Lagrange equation is their equation 10, fourth order in C. We checked all three of those statements symbolically. So their worlds interact through derivatives in the label. Relabelling is a gauge symmetry, and its conserved charge is circulation.

In the centre, Hall, Deckert and Wiseman. Their interworld potential replaces the derivatives in the label by differences between neighbouring worlds. For worlds placed at the quantiles of a unit Gaussian, their potential per world approaches the Fisher value of one eighth as the number of worlds grows: 0.109 with a hundred worlds, and 0.12496 with a hundred thousand. A countable version needs the neighbour graph of the label lattice. In one dimension that graph can be read off the order of the positions; in more dimensions the positions don't determine the neighbours. That's why sorting is a one-dimensional accident.

On the right, our model. The domain is just time and a discrete world index with persistent identity; there's no label manifold. The relation is a function of two target points, the chord's ends, and of the potential there. It needs only midpoints and differences in configuration space. It's linear in W, so it handles mixed and signed states, and nodes cost nothing.

So the answer to my question is no: the relation itself isn't more complicated. It's one functional, like Schiff and Poirier's, and it asks less of the set of worlds. The complication is in realising a signed linear process with finitely many worlds, that is, cancellation and K1 resampling, which is our analogue of Hall, Deckert and Wiseman's neighbour structure.

That leaves one open choice: must a chord's ends be occupied by actual worlds? The generator needs only the potential at those configurations. Schiff and Poirier never face that choice, because their neighbours in the label are always occupied.
