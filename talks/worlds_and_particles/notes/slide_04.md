This is the vocabulary from step 25, Definition WC0, as we amended it in October so that it holds for N particles in three dimensions. The table is the picture I find most useful: the signed ensemble is a matrix whose rows are worlds and whose columns are particles.

A world is a row. It's one complete configuration with its momenta, a point in 6 N dimensions, with a sign, plus or minus one, and a phase, θ, that winds at the Lagrangian rate. Its banks of integrators, one per bond, are now derived rather than postulated, as slide 8 explains. A positon is a world with sign plus one, and a negaton is a world with sign minus one. Positon is short for positon world. Neither is a particle, neither is a hole, and neither has a charge.

A world-particle is a cell: particle i as it appears in world w, a point in six dimensions. It shares its row's sign and clock, and has none of its own.

A particle is a column: the identity that recurs in every world, with its mass and its couplings. It has no state of its own. What we can say about it is the marginal of the signed ensemble on its column, the reduced Wigner function of particle i, seen at the resolution of a cell.

An aligned pair is a positon and a negaton whose clocks agree, and it's dark. An unpaired world is one that isn't half of an aligned pair. And we've retired the word body, which had meant both an unpaired world, in the ledger, and a particle, in steps 21 and 24.

Why did one particle hide all this? With a single particle the table has one column, so every row has exactly one cell. Row, cell and column are then the same thing, and any word works. The old glossary's "a world-particle is a world" is exactly that collapse. With two or more particles the table forces a choice: a sign or a clock belongs to a row, the reduced state belongs to a column, and a cell is only a coordinate of its row.

Entanglement, in this picture, is about how the rows fill the columns jointly: a correlation between the cells of the same row, which no single column can show. I'll come back to that on slide 12.
