Now the first of three tests. If the particles inside a world were formally like worlds, it should be harmless to give each world-particle its own clock and its own reader. In step 25 we did exactly that, and measured the result.

The setting is two particles on a line with a pair potential, and three ways of reading the compensated residual. In W, the world reads its joint chord; that's what the ontology says. In B1, each world-particle reads its own chord, moving only its own coordinate, and its clock carries the whole interaction, as if the other particle were an external field. B2 is the same, but with the interaction split between the two clocks, half each, which is the bookkeeping that makes the world-particle clocks add up to the world's.

Theorem WC2, which we re-checked symbolically, says four things. W and B1 agree at linear order, which the compensated split removes anyway, and they differ from third order on. Exactly, and to all orders, B1's relative motion is the quantum Liouville equation with ħ replaced by ħ/2. B1 leaves the centre-of-mass marginal alone. But it adds a cubic term that couples the centre of mass to the relative motion, which quantum mechanics doesn't have. As for B2, it halves everything, and then reads only half the classical force. In fact, no way of sharing the interaction between the two clocks gives both the right action and Newton's force.

Why ħ/2? A B1 event delivers its momentum ξ to one particle, which moves the relative momentum by only half of ξ, while the pattern of rates is the one-particle kernel at the full chord. A world-particle's reader isn't wrong about the chord. It's wrong about who takes the kick.

The numbers on the slide are measured. From a product state that exact quantum mechanics keeps stationary and separable, W stays separable to 7×10⁻¹⁵ on the mesh, while B1's separability defect reaches 0.2 by time one half. In the Eckart pair collision, W transmits 0.549 and B1 transmits 0.517. The exact values are 0.5441 at ħ, and 0.5120 at ħ/2. So B1 is a good solver of the wrong problem. And in the world ensemble, with integrate-and-fire banks, banks on world-particles carry 2.8 times as many worlds, and 36 times the unsigned mean of P squared, by time 1. They kick every daughter off its parent's centre-of-mass momentum, and heat the ensemble's centre of mass, while the signed marginal stays right on average.

The lesson for the ontology is that the clock belongs to the world, the banks belong to the world's bonds, and nothing belongs to a world-particle. And that lesson can't be learned with one particle, because then the world-particle's clock and the world's clock are the same clock.
