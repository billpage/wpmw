I want to anchor the whole talk on an analogy from classical statistical mechanics, because that field met exactly our confusion more than a century ago, and settled it with two words.

The Ehrenfests named the two spaces in 1912. μ-space is the phase space of one molecule. For a gas of N molecules, the state of the gas is N points in μ-space, and the Boltzmann equation evolves their one-particle density. The molecules interact through a potential, by collisions and mean fields, but all of that happens inside one physical system: one world.

Γ-space is the phase space of the whole system, with 6 N dimensions. One point in Γ-space is the complete microstate of the gas. Gibbs's ensemble, from 1902, is a cloud of such points: imagined copies of the system, which never interact with one another. The Liouville equation moves the cloud.

Our model is a Gibbs ensemble with two changes. The copies carry a sign, and the copies do interact, through the relations on slide 6: chords, readings, emission and absorption. Inside each copy the particles still interact classically, exactly as the molecules do in Boltzmann's gas.

The key point is the line at the bottom of the slide. For one particle, μ-space and Γ-space are the same space. So the world ensemble of a one-particle problem is a cloud in the one-particle phase space, and it looks just like a gas of particles. Every analogy we've used for the sea, Volterra lattices, the chemistry of P–N pairs, four-wave mixing, Cooper pairs, Boltzmann and Landau collisions, is a gas analogy in Boltzmann's sense, applied to an ensemble in Gibbs's sense. With one particle that's harmless. With two or more, it puts the interaction between worlds into the wrong space.

So here's a question for you. When we talk about pair–pair collisions in the sea, or about P–N bound pairs, do we mean points colliding in μ-space, or in Γ-space? For two or more particles only the second makes sense, because a positon and a negaton are worlds. The references for this slide, and for the rest of the talk, are on the last slide.
