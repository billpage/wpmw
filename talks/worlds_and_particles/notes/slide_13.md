The third test is where I have to correct my own framing. The cost of anything that needs two worlds to coincide, whether recombination, annihilation, a contact sink, or finding an absorption partner by search, grows exponentially with the dimension of phase space.

Here's the table. For a Gaussian product state, with cells of half a standard deviation on every phase-space axis, the chance that two independent worlds land in the same cell is about 0.141 per axis, so 0.141^(2dN) in all. Two particles in three dimensions need about 10¹⁰ worlds for each expected partner, and three particles need about 10¹⁵. This is the annihilation problem of full configuration-interaction quantum Monte Carlo, where the sign structure only appears above a critical population, and of the signed-particle Wigner methods: Xiong and Shao's four-dimensional test used 10⁸ bins and more than 10⁹ particles.

The highlighted row is the honest correction. One particle in three dimensions has the same six-dimensional phase space as three particles on a line, and it costs the same. So the cost is an effect of dimension, and your remark on the 13th, that in high dimension a small separation adds up over every coordinate, is the same point. What the one-particle ladder hid was mostly its one-dimensionality.

Now, what the sink does to entanglement. The contact sink removes, in every cell, as many positons and negatons as it can, in equal numbers and at random. It keeps each cell's net weight, but it replaces the shape of W inside the cell with the shape of the majority sign. So it erases interference finer than a cell. It keeps a record of which cell each world is in, and that is decoherence. On problem G, the global purity at resolution one quarter falls 0.14 below the mesh with cells of 0.35, and 0.21 below with cells of 0.7. The deficit doesn't depend on the time step or on how often the sink acts, and with the cell fixed, it doesn't go away as the population grows. For four particles, with six relative dimensions, the worlds keep only 15 per cent of the quantum correction at time 1.

Why does this belong in a talk about worlds and particles? Because the sink is a relation between worlds, which pairs them and removes them, and its cost and its error are set by the dimension of the space the worlds live in, Γ-space, never by the space of one particle. Any analogy that pictures the worlds as a gas in μ-space will badly underestimate it. Your argument that the Wigner function of a Gaussian is nowhere zero, so that every cell is eventually occupied, is right for a density. For any finite population, it's the table that matters.
