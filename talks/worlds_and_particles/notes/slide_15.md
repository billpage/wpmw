The analogy that holds is the configuration-space view of quantum mechanics itself. N particles in d dimensions have a configuration space of d N dimensions and a phase space of 2 d N, and the N-particle Wigner function evolves by the same compensated quantum Liouville equation as one particle would in d N dimensions with that potential. Nothing in the generator knows how the coordinates are grouped into particles. If that's what you mean by a formal analogy, you're right, and everything the one-particle ladder proved about the generator, the compensated split, the soft horizon, the reach as a regulator, the ledger's sum rule, carries over as a statement about one point in a bigger space. That point is the world. A one-particle model, properly understood, was always a model of worlds.

The analogy that misleads takes the particles inside one world and treats them like the worlds of the ensemble, or treats the worlds like a gas of particles in the one-particle phase space, μ-space instead of Γ-space. It's the analogy behind three mistakes we've measured or caught. Giving each particle its own clock and reader, on slide 11, which gives ħ/2, a spurious coupling to the centre of mass, and heating. Reading entanglement as something the events do, on slide 12, when classical streaming inside the worlds already entangles. And estimating the costs of coincidence and of the sink from a picture of worlds as a gas in a low-dimensional space, on slide 13. It also sits behind vocabulary we've had to retire: a world-particle as a world, the word body, and calling a positon or a negaton a particle.

So, to answer the question of slide 2 directly: the one-particle case is not harmful as mathematics. It's harmful as a source of intuition and vocabulary, because with one particle the world and the particle are the same object, and every picture works. For the multi-particle models, the picture has to be the table on slide 4 and the two levels on slide 6.

Here's a test that tells them apart. Ask of any proposed mechanism, a Volterra sea, a bound-pair chemistry, or a rule for pair–pair collisions, whether its objects are rows or columns, worlds or particles, and in which space they meet. If the answer is that it doesn't matter, then the mechanism has only been checked for one particle.
